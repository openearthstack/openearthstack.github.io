How to Detect Floods with SAR.

SAR is the most reliable flood sensor because water backscatters poorly and clouds are transparent. Three methods cover most operational workloads.

Why SAR works

Calm water acts as a specular reflector. The radar signal bounces away from the sensor, returning almost nothing. Flooded land shows up as a dark patch next to the relatively bright dry surface. Clouds and darkness are irrelevant; Sentinel-1 revisits every 6–12 days.

Thresholding

The simplest method: pick a sigma-0 threshold in the VV channel. Pixels below the threshold are water. Works well on flat, unvegetated terrain. Fails under wind (water roughens), in wetlands (mixed pixels), and over dark dry surfaces (tarmac, deserts).

Change detection

Compare a post-event scene against a pre-event baseline. Pixels that became newly dark are flooded. Much more reliable than thresholding because it anchors on local baseline backscatter. Common tooling: GFM (Global Flood Monitoring), HASARD, and various open scripts.

AI classification

Trained classifiers handle the hard cases: flooded vegetation, urban floods where buildings still backscatter, shadow in hilly terrain. Dual-polarisation VV+VH is standard input. Outputs a per-pixel probability with clearer semantics than a threshold.

Pitfalls

  • Rice paddies, aquaculture ponds, and lakes look permanently flooded.
  • High wind roughens water and pushes backscatter above thresholds.
  • Radar shadow over hills looks black but is not water.
  • Urban floods are hard: buildings still scatter strongly, so pure flat-threshold methods miss them.
