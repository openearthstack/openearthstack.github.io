How to Read a SAR Image.

Reading a SAR scene is different from reading an aerial photograph. A few geometric and physical principles decode most of what you see.

Brightness means backscatter

A SAR image encodes radar backscatter, not reflected sunlight. Bright pixels return a lot of the signal; dark pixels return little. Calm water and smooth tarmac look nearly black because the signal reflects away from the sensor. Urban buildings look bright because they act as corner reflectors.

Speckle

The salt-and-pepper noise you see everywhere is speckle, an intrinsic property of coherent imaging. It is multiplicative, not additive, and does not respond to Gaussian denoisers. Multi-looking and SAR-specific filters (Lee, Frost, Refined Lee) handle it.

Geometric distortions

  • Foreshortening: slopes facing the sensor are compressed in range.
  • Layover: the top of a tall feature can return before the bottom, so mountains appear to lean toward the sensor.
  • Shadow: terrain blocked from the radar returns nothing and appears pure black.

Terrain correction (range-Doppler, orthorectification) reduces foreshortening and layover by using a DEM. Shadow cannot be recovered.

Polarisation matters

A VV image and a VH image of the same scene look different. VV is dominated by surface scattering; VH picks up volume scattering in vegetation. Always check which polarisation you are looking at before drawing conclusions.

A practical starting viewer: Sentinel-1 GRD, VV polarisation, in dB, with a 2nd–98th percentile contrast stretch. Most scene context reads at a glance from that setting.
