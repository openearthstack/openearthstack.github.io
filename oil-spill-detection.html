How to Detect Oil Spills.

Oil dampens capillary waves, so a slick appears as a dark patch on the ocean surface. The challenge is separating oil from convincing look-alikes.

Why it works

Ocean surface backscatter on SAR is dominated by small capillary waves. Oil and other surfactants suppress these waves, so a slick returns much less energy than surrounding water. The slick appears as a dark smooth patch on an otherwise textured sea surface.

Wind window

Detection needs wind of roughly 2–6 m/s. Below that, the whole ocean is dark and a slick is invisible. Above that, wind roughens water even inside the slick and contrast disappears.

Workflow

  1. Preprocess a Sentinel-1 EW or IW scene over ocean; land-mask.
  2. Detect dark patches (adaptive thresholding, CFAR, or learned segmentation).
  3. Extract shape and texture features: area, compactness, moments, contrast against surround, edge gradient.
  4. Classify oil vs look-alike.
  5. Attach context: wind field (Sentinel-1 OCN), AIS ship tracks, bathymetry.

Look-alikes to expect

  • Low-wind patches (the most common false positive).
  • Algal blooms and biogenic surfactants.
  • Rain cells (short-lived, circular).
  • Ship wakes (linear, often matched to AIS tracks).
  • Internal waves (wave-like banding).
Operational services like CleanSeaNet (EMSA) combine automated detection with human verification. Modern SAR foundation models narrow the shortlist for the analyst rather than replacing them.
